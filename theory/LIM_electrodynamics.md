# Electrodynamic Modeling of a Planar PCB Linear Induction Motor (LIM)

## 1. Introduction
This document outlines the theoretical framework for a solid-state Linear Induction Motor (LIM) embedded within a multi-layer FR4 Printed Circuit Board. The objective is to calculate the time-averaged Lorentz force exerted on a non-magnetic conductive projectile (aluminum) by a traveling magnetic wave generated by 3-phase stator traces.

## 2. The Traveling Magnetic Wave (Stator Field)
The PCB stator consists of 3-phase interleaved copper traces. When excited by 3-phase sinusoidal currents separated by $120^\circ$ ($2\pi/3$ radians) in time and spatially offset by a wavelength $\lambda$, they produce a linearly traveling magnetic wave.

Let the spatial coordinate along the board be $x$. The primary magnetic flux density in the $z$-direction (perpendicular to the board) is given by:

$$ \mathbf{B}_s(x, t) = B_0 \cos(kx - \omega t)\hat{z} $$

Where:
*   $B_0$ is the peak magnetic flux density (determined by trace current $I$ and turns density).
*   $k = \frac{\pi}{\tau_p}$ is the wave number, where $\tau_p$ is the pole pitch (the physical width of the trace groupings on the PCB).
*   $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency of the SPWM supplied by the inverter.
*   The synchronous velocity of the magnetic wave is $v_s = \frac{\omega}{k} = 2f\tau_p$.

## 3. Magnetic Diffusion and Slip (Rotor Electrodynamics)
As the magnetic wave passes through the aluminum projectile (velocity $v$), the projectile experiences a changing magnetic field. 

We define the **slip** $s$ as the relative difference between the synchronous wave speed and the projectile speed:

$$ s = \frac{v_s - v}{v_s} $$

In the reference frame of the moving projectile, the effective angular frequency of the magnetic field is $\omega_r = s\omega$.

Using **Faraday’s Law of Induction** ($\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$) and **Ohm's Law** for a moving conductor ($\mathbf{J} = \sigma(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})$), the induced Eddy current density $\mathbf{J}$ in the $y$-direction (across the width of the aluminum) is:

$$ J_y = \sigma s v_s B_0 \cos(kx - \omega t) $$

Where $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of 7075-T6 Aluminum.

*(Note: For this initial approximation, we assume the aluminum thickness is less than the magnetic skin depth $\delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu_0 \sigma \omega_r}}$, allowing us to treat the current distribution as uniform across the z-axis).*

## 4. Thrust Calculation (Lorentz Force)
The forward thrust (propulsion) is generated by the interaction of the induced Eddy currents ($\mathbf{J}$) and the applied magnetic field ($\mathbf{B}$). The volumetric Lorentz force density is:

$$ \mathbf{f} = \mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B} $$

Taking the cross product of $J_y \hat{y}$ and $B_z \hat{z}$ yields a force purely in the $x$-direction:

$$ f_x = (\sigma s v_s B_0 \cos(kx - \omega t)) \cdot (B_0 \cos(kx - \omega t)) $$
$$ f_x = \sigma s v_s B_0^2 \cos^2(kx - \omega t) $$

To find the time-averaged force density $\langle f_x \rangle$, we integrate over one period. Since the average of $\cos^2(\theta)$ is $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$ \langle f_x \rangle = \frac{1}{2} \sigma s v_s B_0^2 $$

The total thrust $F_x$ on the projectile of volume $V$ (where $V = \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{thickness}$) is:

$$ F_x = \frac{1}{2} \sigma V s v_s B_0^2 $$

## 5. Engineering Implications for PCB Design
1.  **Thrust is proportional to the slip $s$:** Maximum thrust occurs at $v=0$ (startup), and thrust drops to zero as the projectile reaches the wave speed $v_s$.
2.  **Thrust scales with the square of the magnetic field ($B_0^2$):** Therefore, the trace width in KiCad must be minimized to allow for maximum turns density, and the MOSFET inverter must handle high peak currents to maximize $B_0$.

## 6. Kinematic Equation of Motion and Volume Cancellation
To predict the physical trajectory of the aluminum rotor, we apply Newton's Second Law ($\Sigma F = ma$) in the axis of propagation ($x$).

The mass of the rotor is given by $m = \rho V$, where $\rho$ is the density of the aluminum and $V$ is its volume. Substituting the time-averaged Lorentz force derived in Equation (9):

$$ m \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma V s v_s B_0^2 $$
$$ (\rho V) \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma V \left( \frac{v_s - v}{v_s} \right) v_s B_0^2 $$

Notice that the volume $V$ exists on both sides of the equation and precisely cancels out:

$$ \rho \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma (v_s - v) B_0^2 $$

Rearranging for acceleration $\frac{dv}{dt}$:

$$ \frac{dv}{dt} = \left( \frac{\sigma B_0^2}{2\rho} \right) (v_s - v) $$

This reveals a profound engineering characteristic of the solid-state EMALS system: **In the idealized magnetic diffusion limit, the acceleration of the projectile is independent of its physical dimensions.** 

Letting $\alpha = \frac{\sigma B_0^2}{2\rho}$, we have a first-order linear ordinary differential equation (ODE):

$$ \frac{dv}{dt} + \alpha v = \alpha v_s $$

Assuming the projectile starts from rest ($v(0) = 0$), the exact analytical solution to this ODE is:

$$ v(t) = v_s \left( 1 - e^{-\alpha t} \right) $$

This equation has been discretized and plotted in `simulations/kinematic_solver.py` to dictate the physical length required for the PCB stator in the hardware design phase.
